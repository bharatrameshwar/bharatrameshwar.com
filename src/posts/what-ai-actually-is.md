Everyone is talking about AI. Most of what you hear is either breathless excitement or quiet dread, and very little of it tells you what the thing actually is. So let me try to do that, in plain words, with no jargon and nothing to sell you.

When people say "AI" today, they almost always mean a particular kind of tool: a chatbot you type into, like ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. You write a question or a request, and it writes something back. That is the whole interaction. The interesting part is what happens in between.

## It is a prediction machine, not a brain

Here is the honest version of what these tools do. They predict the next word.

That sounds too simple to be useful, and when I first heard it I did not believe it either. But that is genuinely the core of it. The tool has read an enormous amount of text: books, articles, websites, conversations. From all that reading, it has learned the patterns of how language fits together. When you give it some words, it works out, one piece at a time, what words are likely to come next, given everything it has seen before.

Think of the autocomplete on your phone. You type "I'll be home in five" and it suggests "minutes". That is a tiny version of the same idea. These AI tools are that idea made vastly larger and more capable, trained on so much text that the patterns they have learned are not just "what word comes next in this sentence" but "what would a helpful, sensible answer to this whole question look like".

So when you ask it to write an email, or explain a tax form, or suggest a recipe, it is not looking up an answer in a database. It is generating one, word by word, based on the patterns of every email, explanation, and recipe it has ever seen.

## Why that matters for how you use it

Once you understand that it is predicting rather than knowing, a lot of its behaviour starts to make sense.

It is brilliant at anything that is really a language task. Rewriting something to sound friendlier. Summarising a long document. Explaining a difficult idea in simple terms. Turning your rough notes into a tidy paragraph. These are exactly the things a pattern-of-language machine is good at, and it will often do them better and faster than you could yourself.

It is shakier on hard facts. Because it is generating plausible-sounding text rather than retrieving verified truth, it can state something with total confidence that is simply wrong. The technical world calls this "hallucination", which is a polite way of saying it made something up. It is not lying, because it has no idea it is wrong. It produced the words that fit the pattern, and sometimes the pattern leads somewhere false. This is why you should never trust it blindly on dates, figures, legal details, medical advice, or anything where being wrong has a real cost. Use it, but check it.

It has no memory of you unless you give it one. By default, each conversation starts fresh. It does not know who you are, what you asked yesterday, or what you actually need, unless you tell it in the conversation. That is not a flaw, it is just worth knowing, because it explains why being clear and specific gets you so much further.

## It is a tool, and tools have a knack

I find the most useful way to think about it is as a tool, the way a spreadsheet is a tool. A spreadsheet is enormously powerful, but only if you know roughly what it can do and how to ask it for the right thing. Hand it to someone who has never seen one and they will get very little out of it. Give it ten minutes of guidance and they can do things that would have taken hours by hand.

AI is the same. There is a knack to it, and the knack is learnable. You do not need to understand the engineering underneath, any more than you need to understand how a spreadsheet stores numbers in memory. You just need to know what it is good at, where it falls down, and how to ask.

That is what the rest of these posts are about. Now that you know what the thing actually is, a prediction machine that is wonderful with language and unreliable with facts, the practical advice that follows will make a lot more sense.
