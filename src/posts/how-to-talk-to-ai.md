Most people who are disappointed by AI are disappointed for one reason: they asked it a vague question and got a vague answer back. The tool is only as good as what you give it. The good news is that asking well is not a technical skill. It is closer to the way you would brief a capable new assistant on their first day. You would not just say "sort out the trip". You would tell them where, when, the budget, and what matters to you. AI is exactly the same.

Here are the habits that make the difference. None of them are clever. All of them work.

## Give it the context it cannot guess

The tool knows nothing about your situation unless you tell it. So tell it.

A weak request: "Write a message asking for a refund."

A strong one: "Write a polite but firm email asking for a refund. I bought a kettle from an online shop three weeks ago, it stopped working after two days, and they have ignored my first two emails. I want a full refund, not a replacement. Keep it short."

The second one gets you something you can almost send as-is, because you gave it the facts it needs. The first one gets you a generic template you then have to rewrite. The effort you spend explaining the situation is repaid several times over in the quality of what comes back.

## Tell it who it is talking to and how long to be

Two small additions change the whole tone of an answer.

Say who the audience is. "Explain this to me like I have never studied science" gives you a very different answer from "explain this to a fellow engineer". Neither is better in general, but one of them is right for you. The tool will happily pitch itself at any level, but only if you tell it which.

Say how long you want it. Left to its own devices, these tools tend to waffle. If you want a short answer, ask for one. "In two sentences", "as a short list", "just the bottom line, no preamble". You are allowed to be that direct, and it helps enormously.

## Show it the raw material

You do not have to describe something when you can just paste it in. This is the habit that unlocks the most everyday value.

Paste in the confusing letter from the council and ask "what is this actually asking me to do, in plain English". Paste in your rough notes and ask it to turn them into a tidy paragraph. Paste in the long email thread and ask "what did we actually agree, and what am I meant to do next". Paste in the ingredients you have in the fridge and ask what you could cook.

Working from your real material, it gives you a real answer about your situation, not a generic one about situations like yours.

## Treat it as a conversation, not a vending machine

This is the habit people most often miss. The first answer is a starting point, not the final word. You can talk back.

If the email it wrote is too formal, say "make it warmer and less stiff". If the explanation went over your head, say "that was still too complicated, try again more simply". If it gave you ten options and you only wanted three, say so. Each time you push back, it adjusts. A few rounds of "not quite, more like this" will get you somewhere far better than the first attempt, and it costs you nothing but a sentence.

I almost never use the first thing it gives me. I treat the whole thing as a back-and-forth, the way I would with a colleague, and that is where the good results come from.

## A simple shape to remember

If you want one thing to take away, it is this rough shape for a good request:

- **What you want.** "Write an email", "explain this", "give me ideas for".
- **The context it needs.** The facts of your actual situation.
- **Who it is for and how long.** The audience and the length.
- **The raw material, if you have any.** Pasted straight in.

You will not always need all four. But when an answer comes back useless, it is almost always because one of them was missing. Add it, ask again, and watch the quality jump.

That really is most of the skill. Not magic words, not secret tricks, just being clear about what you want and willing to ask again. In the next post, I will show some specific everyday things you can do with all this, the small uses that quietly make a week easier.
